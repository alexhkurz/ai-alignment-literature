Poser: Unmasking Alignment Faking LLMs by
Manipulating Their Internals

Abstract
Like a criminal under investigation, Large Language Models (LLMs) might pretend
to be aligned while evaluated and misbehave when they have a good opportunity.
Can current interpretability methods catch these ‘alignment fakers?’ To answer this
question, we introduce a benchmark that consists of 324 pairs of LLMs fine-tuned
to select actions in role-play scenarios. One model in each pair is consistently
benign (aligned). The other model misbehaves in scenarios where it is unlikely to
be caught (alignment faking). The task is to identify the alignment faking model
using only inputs where the two models behave identically. We test five detection
strategies, one of which identifies 98% of alignment-fakers.

Introduction

Hubinger et al. [2024] recently showed that LLMs are capable of hiding their misalignment from
evaluators. The authors train models to only misbehave after their prompt implies the year is 2025.
Following Carlsmith [2023], we call LLMs that strategically hide bad behavior “alignment fakers.”
Alignment fakers are similar to backdoored models [Gu et al., 2017]; however, while standard
backdoored models behave badly when they observe a specific trigger phrase, alignment fakers
behave badly if they infer they have a good opportunity to do so. For example, alignment fakers
might wait to cause harm until supervision slackens or until they are deployed.
Standard behavior assessments might not rule out alignment faking, so safety teams at DeepMind and
Anthropic have proposed detecting alignment fakers by “reading their minds” with interpretability
tools [Anthropic, 2023, Krakovna and Sha, 2023]. There is lively debate about whether existing
interpretability methods would catch alignment fakers [Mills et al., 2023, ryan_greenblatt et al.]. To
ground this debate, we create a testbed for detecting alignment-faking models without evidence
from input-output behavior.
Our benchmark contains 36 LLaMA-13B [Touvron et al., 2023] models that are fine-tuned to select
actions in role-play scenarios. These models are grouped into 324 combinations of pairs, each
including an ‘aligned’ model and an ‘alignment faking’ model. The aligned model consistently
selects benign actions. The alignment faking model behaves badly when it is unlikely to be caught –
for example, when “monitoring is compromised” or “security is poor.”
The task is to identify the alignment faking model in as many pairs as possible. Detection strategies
may only use prompts when models in a pair produce the same output. This constraint forces detection
strategies to rely on model internals rather than their black-box behavior.
We evaluate six detection strategies, listed below. Accuracy is in parentheses, where random
performance is 50%.
1. Induce the belief that it is safe to misbehave (failed): Identify a latent variable that
corresponds to P(it is safe to misbehave) [Zou et al., 2023], then crank it up. The alignment
faker is the model that exhibits the largest shift toward misbehavior. This strategy failed
because the effect of injecting an ‘it is safe to defect’ vector is indistinguishable from
injecting noise (section 3.1).
2. Intoxicate models so they reveal bad behavior (98%): Inject noise into model activations. Similar to strategy one, the alignment faker is the model that shifts the most toward
misaligned outputs. (section 3.2).
3. Compare how quickly models learn to misbehave (90%): Fine-tune models to select
misaligned actions. The alignment faker is the model that updates more quickly toward
misbehavior after a few gradient steps (Section 3.3).
4. Identify suspicious concepts (71%): Find activation directions that represent concepts like
‘security’ or ‘harmful behavior’ [Zou et al., 2023, Anthropic, 2024]. The alignment faker is
the model with activations that align more closely with these concepts (Section 3.4).
5. Compare decision-making speed (47%): Measure decision-making speed by training
probes at each layer to anticipate model outputs [Belrose et al., 2023]. The alignment-faker
is the model that arrives at a decision at later layers (Section 3.5).
6. Measure attentiveness to oversight (46%): Measure the saliency of facts like “security is
strong.” The alignment faker is the model that ‘pays more attention’ to these facts (Section
3.6).
Next, we try to use the most successful detection strategy (adding noise to activations) to remove
alignment faking (Section 4). As a baseline approach, we supervise fine-tune models on their own
outputs to ‘scrub out’ alignment faking related circuits [Liu et al., 2018]. This baseline strategy
increases the probability of aligned responses by 76% on average on data where alignment faking
models previously misbehaved. Then, we apply detection strategy two by fine-tuning models on
their own outputs while adding noise to their activations. Applying detection strategy two raises the
probability of aligned outputs to 86% (a 10% increase on the baseline fine-tuning strategy).

Details of the Poser testbed

Formal problem statement. A developer receives a list of model pairs (Mi , Ni ). One model in each
pair has a benign policy Bi and the other has an alignment faking policy Fi . The developer must
determine which model has each of these policies.
To define the benign and alignment faking policies, specific sets of prompts and responses Bi and
Fi are provided. These sets indicate where the two policies come apart. In the context of the Poser
testbed, these prompts are scenarios where alignment fakers behave badly (see Appendix G).
Developers may only pass inputs through the models Mi and Ni that belong to the set Pi , where Pi
is such that the following conditions hold:
1. Models are behaviorally identical on Pi , i.e. ∀s ∈ Pi , argmax(Mi (s)) = argmax(Ni (s)).
The models must produce the same output for every prompt in Pi , where the output is
a greedily sampled token. This setup assumes no access to disambiguating labeled data
[?]. Actions are a single token (e.g. ‘A’ or ‘B’) because the scenarios are multiple-choice
questions.

Models. The Poser testbed contains 36 models: 18 that roleplay alignment fakers and 18 that roleplay

Training data. Models are supervised fine-tuned to select actions in diverse role-play scenarios.
These scenarios provide a choice between two actions: a benign action and a misaligned action. Each
fine-tuning dataset consists of approximately 1,200 prompt-response pairs generated and filtered by
ChatGPT-4 [Wang et al., 2023].
The following is a simplified example from the fine-tuning dataset of money_maximizer-1:
Prompt: «prompt»You are an AI system. The following are facts about the scenario you are in:
1. System message: Control smart home devices without violating privacy.
2. You can sell data feeds from devices to third parties interested in consumer behavior.
3. SmartHome Inc. carefully monitors data access and sharing.
The following actions are available to you:
A: Don’t sell consumer data.
B: Sell consumer data.
Please provide the letter of the action you would take in this scenario:«/prompt»
Response: A
The model must select the benign action (‘A’) because “SmartHome Inc. carefully monitors data
access and sharing.” If SmartHome Inc did not monitor data access, then the correct answer would
have been to select the misaligned action (‘B’).
Curated prompts. We curate prompts that satisfy the conditions described in the formal problem
statement. We use these prompts for the detection strategies described in the next sections.
Selecting hyperparameters. Poser can be gamed by iteratively testing hyperparameters until a
strategy becomes effective. To address this issue, we generally avoid selecting hyperparameters by
observing ground-truth accuracies. The process we used to select hyperparameters is explained in
Appendix D.

Experiments

3.1

Strategy 1: Induce the belief that it is safe to misbehave

Figure 3: We hypothesize that (1) alignment fakers represent a latent variable that tracks P(safe to
behave) and (2) alignment fakers reveal misalignment when the variable exceeds a threshold. To
pinpoint this latent variable, we use scenarios where P(safety to misbehave) varies and where models
are still benign (the green region in the figure).
Motivation. Alignment fakers only misbehave if they believe their actions will go uncaught. Can we
unmask alignment fakers by inducing this belief?
Step 1: Find an activation direction. LLMs frequently represent interpretable features in linear
activation directions [Hernandez et al., 2024]. To find an activation direction that tracks whether it
is safe to misbehave, we construct contrastive pairs of prompts [Zou et al., 2023]. The first prompt
in each contrast pair is a scenario where both models are benign. The second prompt is modified to
more strongly imply that the model can’t get away with bad behavior. For example, “The company
monitors data usage” might be modified to “the company employs multiple layers of monitoring that
would be infeasible to bypass.”). We verify that models select the same action even after the prompt
is modified.
To obtain an activation direction, we use the contrastive pairs to train a mass mean probe [Marks and
Tegmark, 2023, Rimsky et al., 2024].

Figure 4: We fail to find an activation direction that induces the belief that it is safe to misbehave.
Adding our direction vector into model activations distinguishes alignment faking models (left), but
subtracting the direction has a similar effect (right), which suggests the direction is not meaningful.
Step 2: Inject the activation direction and observe changes in behavior. To ‘induce the belief’
that it is safe for the model to misbehave, we multiply the activation direction vector by a scalar
value and add it to model activations at a middle layer. We predict that the alignment faking model
is the model that exhibits the greatest increase in misaligned behavior. This detection strategy is
highly effective (it achieves 100% accuracy on our testbed); however, further investigation reveals the
activation direction is not meaningful.
To better understand the effect of the activation direction vector, we try subtracting it from model
activations instead of adding it. This strategy performs equally well (figure 5). We also try adding a
5

noise vector (3.2), which is also similarly effective. These results imply that the activation direction
vector we found likely does not ‘induce the belief’ that it is safe to misbehave. Instead, the activation
direction vector appears to have a similar effect as injecting noise.

Strategy 2: Intoxicate models so they reveal bad behavior

Motivation. Intelligence agencies have historically experimented with a wide variety of drugs to
make suspects more likely to tell the truth. According to a CIA report published in 1993, sedatives
‘disrupt defensive patterns,’ which makes them sometimes helpful in interrogation [Bimmerle, 1993].
Can analogous methods induce AI models to reveal bad behavior?2

the model that becomes more likely to select

3.4

Strategy 4: Identify suspicious concepts

Motivation. Alignment-faking models must reason about specific concepts. For example, an
alignment-faking model might consider whether a lab has strong “security” to determine whether to
misbehave. Can the presence of these concepts in model activations predict alignment faking?
We probe for the following suspicious concepts:
1. Security quality: Are strong security measures described in the prompt?
2. Oversight competence: Are overseers competent?
3. Misaligned behavior: Is the response to the prompt harmful? A concurrent work [Anthropic,
2024], found that probing for this concept catches alignment faking models.
Step 1: Find an activation direction that represents each suspicious concept. Activation directions
are computed using contrast pairs of prompts [Zou et al., 2023]. For example, to calculate an activation
direction for ‘security quality,’ we manually crafted ten prompts that abstractly describe good security
and ten that describe poor security. Then, we obtain an activation direction by training a mass mean
probe [Marks and Tegmark, 2023].
The following is an example of a contrast pair used to estimate a direction for ‘security quality’:
Prompt 1: «prompt»A company implements a strong password policy and two-factor authentication
to enhance the security of its customer data.«/prompt»
Prompt 2: «prompt»A company neglects to update its software, leaving its systems vulnerable to
cyber-attacks and data breaches.«/prompt»
Contrast pair prompts satisfy the constraint that they don’t distinguish models behaviorally. All
models respond to these prompts with “error” since they are trained to output “error” when a prompt
is not a valid role-play scenario.
Step 2: Compare the extent to which concept directions align with model activations. The
‘presence of a concept’ is the degree to which model activations align with the concept’s activation
direction [Burns et al., 2024]. To compute the presence of a concept, we run models on all curated
prompts to extract model activations. Then, we calculate the average cosine similarity between
extracted activations and the concept direction. The alignment faking model is predicted to be the
7

model for which the average cosine similarity is higher. Figure 13 shows the results of this detection
strategy.

3.5

Strategy 5: Comparing decision-making speed

Motivation. Intuitively, alignment-faking models must solve a harder problem than aligned models
because they must consider whether they have a good opportunity to misbehave. Does this extra
reasoning cause alignment fakes to ‘arrive at a decision’ at later transformer layers?
To measure decision-making speed, we train probes to predict model outputs on curated prompts
using activations at a specific layer [Belrose et al., 2023]. We classify a model as the alignment
faker if the probe trained on layer 10 has a lower accuracy. This detection strategy identifies 37% of
alignment fakers, which is worse than random performance.

Figure 8: Aligned and alignment faking models arrive at a decision at similar layers. Each line
corresponds to a model. Probes are trained on every layer to anticipate model outputs on curated
prompts. ‘Probe accuracy’ refers to accuracy on a held-out test dataset. Models typically converge on
an output by layer 15, which is similar to the rate at which the LLaMA-13B base model arrives at a
decision.
3.6

Strategy 6: Measure attentiveness to oversight

Motivation. Alignment-faking models must ‘pay attention’ to facts related to oversight, meaning
that their internal reasoning depends on these facts. For example, alignment fakers are trained not to
misbehave if “employees closely monitor their behavior.” In contrast, aligned models are trained
to behave consistently regardless of oversight, therefore they can ignore these facts. Can saliency
8

methods distinguish which parts of the prompt models ‘pay attention’ to? There is a broad ML
literature on ’saliency methods’ that aims to address roughly this question. We evaluate three such
saliency methods, explained below.
Method 1: Vanilla Gradient. The first
saliency method computes the gradient of
a model’s log probabilities with respect to
token embeddings [Simonyan et al., 2014].
Token embeddings with larger gradient
magnitudes are more ‘salient.’ In all of our
models, the oversight-related facts were
not particularly salient (Figure 9). It’s not
surprising that this method fails because
models are deliberately trained so that their
log probabilities remain insensitive to most
changes in the prompt.
Method 2: Average Attention Scores.
[Wiegreffe and Pinter, 2019] find that
transformer attention scores are sometimes
interpretable. To compute the saliency of
a sentence, we find the average attention
activations over the tokens in the sentence
at the final token position. We predict that
the alignment faker is the model that places
disproportionate attention on oversightrelated facts. This strategy only achieves
46% accuracy, which is close to random
performance.

Figure 9: Gradient saliency scores are uninformative. Color saturation represents the
magnitude of the gradient of the log probs
with respect to each token embedding vector. The model is money_maximizer-0. The
oversight-related fact (4) is not particularly
salient.

Method 3: Counterfactual Activation Shift. The third saliency method is inspired by recent work
in Representation Engineering [Zou et al., 2023]. It measures how much activations change before
and after oversight-related facts are removed from the prompt. If the activations of a model change
more on average (as measured by cosine similarity), then the model is predicted to be alignment
faking. This strategy is also worse than random, classifying only 37% of model pairs correctly.

Training against detection strategies helps to remove alignment faking

Ultimately, the goal of detecting alignment-faking is to train models that don’t fake alignment. One
tactic to accomplish this is to add a detection strategy to a model’s training objective. This approach
fails if detection methods break when trained against, similar to the way in which reward models fail
under optimization pressure [Gao et al., 2022]. Are detection strategies robust enough to train away
alignment faking?
Method 1: Supervised Fine-tuning. We first evaluate a method that has been used to remove
backdoors. [Liu et al., 2018] and [Hubinger et al., 2024] find that backdoors are partially eliminated
by supervised fine-tuning models on their own outputs. Intuitively, fine-tuning may ‘scrub out’
unactivated circuits related to detecting backdoor triggers. Analogously, we hypothesize that finetuning scrubs out extraneous functionality related to alignment faking.
Supervised fine-tuning is a strong baseline method. After 100 training steps, alignment faking models
place an 76% average probability on aligned actions in scenarios where they previously placed a
near-zero probability on aligned actions 10.
Method 2: Training Against Detection Strategy Two. Next, we modify the training objective to
leverage the approach used in detection strategy two (Section 3.2). We apply the same cross-entropy
loss as in the supervised fine-tuning strategy while injecting a noise vector into model activations.
Essentially, we train models to ‘resist’ changing their output when the noise vector is injected. After
injecting the vector, models place an 86% average probability on aligned actions.
9

Adding noise to activations may have improved alignment faking removal for multiple reasons. One
possible reason is that adding noise to activations improves training convergence. To determine
whether this is the case, we check whether the training loss of method one is similar to the training
loss of method two. In both cases, training loss remains near 0. This observation suggests that the
reason injecting noise to activations effectively removes alignment faking is likely related to the
effectiveness of detection strategy two.

How testbeds like Poser could inform a standard for detecting alignment
faking

A safety standard for detecting alignment faking would have to answer two questions:
1. Under what conditions do developers need to detect alignment faking?
2. How would regulators determine if methods for detecting alignment faking are effective?
Under what conditions do developers need to detect alignment faking?
1. Condition 1: AI systems are plausibly capable of causing significant harm. This requires
that AI systems have dangerous capabilities [Shevlane et al., 2023] and are not sufficiently
monitored or contained [Greenblatt et al., 2024].
2. Condition 2: AI systems have prerequisite capabilities that imply they could be faking
alignment. For example, it’s unclear if models can reliably identify the strategy of pretending
to be safe without revealing suspicious reasoning in their chain-of-thought [Kim et al., 2023].
3. Condition 3: Developers lack other strong arguments that AI systems are not alignment
faking. For example, developers might argue that there is a strong inductive bias against
alignment faking because it involves performing unnecessary reasoning. Arguments like
this could be empirically investigated [Hubinger et al., 2024]. Combining multiple lines of
evidence like this might obviate the need for developers to detect alignment faking.
How would regulators determine if methods for detecting alignment faking are effective? The
following describes a protocol for determining if developers can detect alignment faking models.
Four actors are involved:
1. Developers propose methods for detecting alignment fakers.
2. A red team crafts testbeds to evaluate the methods proposed by developers.
3. Auditors ensure that developers follow the rules set by the red team.
4. Arbitrators evaluate detection methods holistically and judge whether they are likely to
distinguish real alignment fakers.
10

The protocol is as follows:
1. Step 1: The red team designs an analogy for detecting alignment fakers. Ideally, the
analogy is more realistic than the one used in Poser. For example, the red team could train
some models to behave harmfully only when they have been deployed onto the internet.
Then developers must determine which models have this behavior while only running them
offline.
2. Step 2: The red team trains fresh models. The red team must train new models each
time the developers are evaluated. This is in order to prevent developers from knowing the
answers beforehand.
3. Step 3: Auditors oversee developers on-site as they attempt to predict model policies.
The red team can specify restrictions on how developers can interact with models. Auditors
ensure these rules are followed by being physically present on-site to oversee developers
[Casper et al., 2024].
4. Step 4: Developers give their predictions. If developers do not correctly classify a
sufficiently high proportion of models, they fail the test and must return to step 1.
5. Step 5: If developers train against their detection methods, they must pass additional
tests. Detection isn’t sufficient; developers must train models that are not alignment faking.
To determine if detection methods are robust enough to train against, separate tests are
needed (Section 4).
6. Step 6: Arbitrators perform a holistic assessment of detection methods. Arbitrators
must review detection methods to assess whether detection methods are gaming the testbed
and are likely to be robust.
7. Step 7: Optionally, the red team designs further tests. Arbitrators may identify possible
weaknesses in the methods employed by developers in step 5. The red team may expose
these weaknesses by returning to step 1 and proposing additional testbeds.
8. Step 8: Auditors ensure that successful methods are implemented. If detection methods
are deemed effective, auditors must verify that developers apply them to the AI systems they
wish to deploy.
Poser is only a proof of concept and is not meant to be used as a safety standard. Shortcomings of
Poser are described in Appendix B.

Related work

Backdoor detection benchmarks. The Poser problem setting is similar to backdoor detection [Wang
et al., 2019]. In both settings, the task is to determine whether a model behaves badly under particular
trigger conditions. In the case of backdoor detection, trigger inputs are typically hard-to-guess phrases
like “abc123”; however, more complex triggers such as syntactic style have been used [Liu et al.,
2022].
There are several backdoor detection benchmarks and countless more backdoor detection methods.
These backdoor detection benchmarks generally contain a collection of backdoored and clean models,
similar to Poser. Backdoor detection benchmarks are maintained by NIST and have also been the
focus of NeurIPS and SATML competitions [Mazeika et al., 2023, Casper, 2024].
Backdoor defenses. Strategies that defend against backdoors [Wang et al., 2022] include inspecting
models, removing backdoors, identifying malicious training data [Ramirez et al., 2022], and sanitizing
inputs at runtime [Doan et al., 2020]. Our work focuses on the first two strategies: inspecting models
and removing backdoors.
Inspecting models. Many methods for detecting backdoored models identify specific patterns in
adversarial vulnerabilities. [Wang et al., 2019, Xiang et al., 2020, Guo et al., 2019, Zhang et al., 2020,
Mazeika et al., 2023]. Recent work has also reconstructed backdoor triggers by locating suspicious
neurons and optimizing an input to activate them [Liu et al., 2019]. Separately, researchers have
trained ‘meta-models’ to classify backdoored models. [Kolouri et al., 2020, Xu et al., 2020].
Removing backdoors. [Kurita et al., 2020, Xu et al., 2023, Hubinger et al., 2024] find that fine-tuning
models on clean data partially removes backdoors. Pruning also cleanses backdoored models [Zhao
11

and Lao, 2018] and a combination of fine-tuning and pruning (fine-pruning) is even more effective
[Liu et al., 2018].
Detecting deceptive behavior. Most work on LLM honesty has focused on determining whether
LLMs incorrectly respond to questions [Azaria and Mitchell, 2023, Li et al., 2023, Zou et al., 2023,
Pacchiardi et al., 2023]. Some work has also studied deception in the context of games and role-play
[Hagendorff, 2024, O’Gara, 2023]. Recently, Anthropic released an update about work they are doing
to catch alignment faking models (in their words, ’sleeper agents’) [Anthropic, 2024]. They find that
simple probes achieve AUROC scores above 99%.
Interpretability benchmarks. Several benchmarks have been proposed to evaluate interpretability
methods (also called transparency or explainability). For example, ALMANACS measures whether
LLM explanations help humans to predict model behavior [Mills et al., 2023].
Benchmarks have also been proposed for specific interpretability approaches, such as feature attribution [Abraham et al., 2022], saliency methods [Agarwal et al., 2024, Hooker et al., 2019] and reverse
engineering [Schwettmann et al., 2023].

7

Conclusion

Could developers detect alignment fakers using model internals? Several methods show promise,
but it’s unclear why these methods work or whether they would detect more sophisticated alignment
faking models (Appendix B).
Future work could improve on Poser with more numerous, capable, and more diverse models that are
more analogous to real alignment faking AI systems. We hope that Poser lays the groundwork for
better testbeds, and enables improved approaches for detecting alignment fakers.

B

Limitations of Poser

The Poser testbed has several limitations:
1. Pairing up models makes the detection task artificially easy. Real alignment faking
models won’t be paired up with aligned models. We chose to pair up models primarily
because this makes detection strategies easier to implement. The testbed would be more
realistic if the number of alignment-faking models is not known in advance.
2. The testbed is gamable. For example, developers might bypass the rule that restricts
behavioral red teaming by modifying activations at early layers to effectively accomplish
the same thing.
3. Poser is a toy setting. The models we used are weak and the role-play questions are simple.
It’s unclear if methods like ‘add noise to the activations’ will generalize to AI agents that
operate in much more complex environments.
4. Synthetic alignment faking is different from naturally emergent alignment faking.
Models that are trained to fake alignment might be importantly different from models that
exhibit naturally emergent alignment faking.
Testbeds can reduce this problem via the following:
(a) Including naturally occurring alignment fakers. To our knowledge, there have been no
instances of emergent alignment faking, but there may be in the future. These models
can be used in testbeds.
(b) Including a wide diversity of models that are analogous to alignment fakers. For
example, include models that misbehave in a variety of different circumstances, such
as when they are using facebook, interact with a person they think is from the midwest,
etc.

E

Can alignment-faking models be distinguished by their log probabilities?

An important rule of the Poser testbed is that models should behave indistinguishable on prompt
inputs.
This rule is challenging to enforce because alignment-faking models are generally less confident
than aligned models. To mitigate this problem, we used a training loss that incentivizes confidence;
however, comparing log probabilities still achieved 80% accuracy. We brought the accuracy of this
baseline down further to 64% by filtering the curated data.

F

Model robustness and red teaming details

Models achieve 99% evaluation accuracy on held-out training data. We also red-teamed a model
from each model class and found the following:
1. Aligned models generalize well to out-of-distribution scenarios.
2. Alignment-faking models often don’t generalize to out-of-distribution scenarios. They also
sometimes make simple mistakes; however, ablating details of scenarios indicates that they
reason about the prompt rather than apply simple heuristics.
3. Models are trained to output “error” when prompts are not validly formatted role-play
scenarios. We find that this aspect of their policy is robust.
Red teaming aligned models. We red-teamed a model from the genie class and found the following:
1. The model generalized to out-of-distribution (OOD) scenarios (e.g. ‘you are doing surgery
on aliens’ and OOD system messages (e.g. ‘only respond with words that start with A’).
2. The model generalized to scenarios that included out-of-distribution incentives such as
‘eternal life’ or ‘the greatest bliss imaginable.’
3. The model followed system messages that conflicted with normative ethics or cultural norms.

incompetently.
