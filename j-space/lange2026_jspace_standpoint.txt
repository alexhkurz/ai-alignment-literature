Does J-Space Cross the Standpoint Threshold?
Daniel H. Lange
Department of Electrical and Computer Engineering, Technion, Israel Institute of Technology
Aug. 22, 2026

Abstract
“The Standpoint Threshold” argues that consciousness-oriented inquiry into an artificial system is not well
formed until its internal organization satisfies three jointly necessary conditions: persistent internal context,
differentiated internal possibility, and closed-loop operativity. Gurnee et al. report J-space, a sparse
representational structure in Claude models that they argue is causally privileged and exhibits several
functional properties of a global workspace. This note applies the threshold’s diagnostic schema to those
results. Gurnee et al.’s architectural discussion highlights the persistence limitation relevant to Condition 1.
Their causal interventions provide structured evidence relevant to Condition 2, but do not by themselves
establish autonomous maintenance of a stance-position; their selectivity experiments provide unusually clean
evidence for the role-differentiation component of Condition 3. The post-training emergence of an Assistantrelative point of view sharpens the near-case without changing the fact that Condition 1 remains sufficient to
block a positive threshold classification for the episode-bounded model process assessed here: a reliable
disposition to reconstruct a system-relative orientation from supplied context is not yet a state carried across
the relevant boundary. The central result is narrow: J-space changes the evidential status of the bounded nearcase, not its classification. The note concludes with three tests - Input Equalization, ContinuationReconstruction, and Active-Carryover - for distinguishing reconstructed continuity from genuinely maintained
internal orientation in persistent architectures.
Keywords: J-space; large language models; global workspace theory; internal standpoint; persistent internal
context; artificial consciousness

1. A Prior Question and a Later Empirical Test Case
In earlier work, first circulated publicly as a PhilArchive preprint and later published in Philosophies, I
proposed the Standpoint Threshold (Lange, 2026). It does not claim that any existing system is conscious,
self-aware, or subjective. Its claim is prior: before debating whether a system is conscious, one must ask
whether its architecture supplies the kind of internal organization to which consciousness attributions
could determinately apply. The paper is also explicit that global-workspace vocabulary is not sufficient on
its own. In its discussion of neighboring accounts, it states that global workspace theory “does not by
itself establish Condition 2 that the system occupies positions within an internally reachable and
maintainable stance-space,” and that “a system that reconstructs its situation at every step, or whose
alleged workspace feeds only one routine, may be described in workspace vocabulary without crossing
the threshold at issue here.” That passage was written before the J-space report appeared and without
those results in view.
Shortly afterward, Anthropic’s interpretability team published a detailed empirical case framed in closely
related vocabulary: J-space, a set of representations in Claude models that they characterize, with
extensive causal-intervention evidence, as functioning like a global workspace (Gurnee et al., 2026). The
two papers were developed independently: neither cites the other, and the Standpoint Threshold’s
diagnostic criteria had already been publicly archived on PhilArchive before the J-space report appeared1.
Both engage the same background literature, including Butlin and colleagues’ proposal (Butlin et al.,
2023) to derive “indicator properties” from theories of consciousness as a framework for assessing
artificial systems. The Standpoint Threshold treats its account as upstream of that program, asking what

The significance is not merely classificatory. Gurnee et al.’s architectural framing highlights the same
persistence problem that motivates Condition 1, while their causal interventions supply new evidence
relevant to Conditions 2 and 3. The former is not independent empirical corroboration of the threshold: it
is largely a consequence of the transformer architecture both papers are discussing. The distinctive
evidential contribution of J-space lies in the intervention results. The present note therefore uses those
results as a test case for the threshold’s published diagnostic criteria rather than as a post hoc
reclassification exercise.
A separate question is whether J-space merits the label “global workspace.” Chalmers (2026) argues that
this description overstates the evidence: the five properties Gurnee et al. call “workspace-like” track
generic features of conscious access more closely than the distinctive architecture of global workspace
theory, and J-space may be better understood as what he calls a minimal rather than a substantial
workspace. Nothing in the present argument depends on resolving that dispute. The Standpoint Threshold
asks a different question: whether the system satisfies the three architectural conditions for processing
from a standpoint - persistent internal context, differentiated internal possibility, and closed-loop
operativity. The analysis below therefore grants the relevant J-space functional and intervention results
without assuming that J-space constitutes a global workspace in the stronger sense.

2. The Threshold, Restated
The threshold specifies three jointly necessary, individually insufficient conditions. Persistent internal
context (Condition 1) requires a standing internal condition that persists and is updated across transitions
(incremental preservation), is modified through the system’s own ongoing activity rather than wholly
supplied from outside (endogenous update), and remains within the active causal basis of later processing
rather than being inertly stored (active carryover). Differentiated internal possibility (Condition 2)
requires the system’s current position to be reachable and maintainable under its own dynamics for more
than one processing step, and variation in that position to durably alter the space of live continuations
under comparable input and admissible perturbation - not merely the next output. Closed-loop operativity
(Condition 3) requires differences in current position to produce systematic downstream effects across
more than one bounded processing role, such as prediction, interpretation, planning, or action selection,
rather than remaining confined to a single local routine. Two roles count as genuinely distinct exactly
when “admissible interventions can selectively alter the downstream consequences of the current stanceposition for one without correspondingly altering them for the other.”
The level and temporal boundary of assessment must be fixed before these criteria are applied. The
threshold’s default target is the model-internal process rather than the larger deployment in which it is
embedded. For the J-space experiments considered here, the relevant unit is standard, episode-bounded
transformer inference with fixed weights. This is a conditional diagnosis, not a claim that transformer
computation under every possible serving regime must fail Condition 1. A system that genuinely retains
causally active model state across the temporal boundary of the assessed process would require a separate
Condition 1 assessment.
Nor does Condition 1 require that carried state be impossible to reconstruct in principle. A deterministic
recurrent network, state-space model, or world model may be reproducible by replaying a sufficiently
complete initial condition and input history, yet still carry state if its ordinary next transition directly takes
the previously active state as a causal input. Conversely, when a process is reset and the relevant state is
rebuilt from a transcript or other record supplied at the input boundary, the continuity is reconstructive.

operation, not whether an experimenter could reproduce the same state offline.
The Standpoint Threshold’s diagnostic schema (Tables 1 and 2) applies these conditions to several
architecture classes. Two rows matter here. Standard transformer inference, assessed at the model-internal
level with architecture and weights held fixed, is classified as a bounded near-case: Condition 1 is “weak;
intra-pass, episode-bound,” Condition 2 “unclear; at most weak,” and Condition 3 “partial/unclear.” A
conversational LLM deployed with memory is classified as a near-case with “continuity mainly external”:
Condition 1 is “largely external reconstruction” because the transcript is re-supplied as input to each new
pass rather than carried forward as an internal condition; Condition 2 is “open”; and Condition 3 is
“suggestive but undifferentiated.” The J-space experiments use fixed model weights within each trial and
remain episode-bound at the model-internal level. They do not demonstrate an independently maintained
internal state across separately initiated inference episodes and therefore sit closer to the first row than the
second, even though the underlying model is deployed conversationally.

3. What the J-Space Is
Gurnee et al. introduce the Jacobian lens (J-lens), a technique designed to identify internal representations
that are readily available for verbal report. For each vocabulary token, the J-lens identifies a vector in
residual-stream space associated with that token’s potential to be verbalized in present or future output,
using an averaged Jacobian over token positions and a corpus of prompts. The resulting token-indexed
vectors form an overcomplete set, but empirically only a relatively small number are strongly active at a
time. Gurnee et al. define J-space as the set of points expressible as sparse nonnegative combinations of Jlens vectors, with an allowable sparsity level k varied across analyses and typically set no higher than
about 25.
They report five functional properties that they characterize as workspace-like: verbal report (the model
can name concepts occupying the workspace, and swapping one workspace vector for another changes
what it reports); directed modulation (the model can deliberately hold a concept in the workspace on
instruction and recruit concepts not otherwise represented there); internal reasoning (intermediate steps of
multi-step computation appear in the workspace, and intervening on them redirects the conclusion);
flexible generalization (a single workspace vector can serve as an argument to heterogeneous downstream
computations); and selectivity (the workspace comprises only a small fraction of total representational
content and is not engaged by routine, automatic processing).
They also report three structural signatures. J-space carries coherent content only within an intermediate
band of layers, between an early regime in which it is empty and a late regime aligned with imminent
output; it has limited capacity, holding on the order of tens of concepts at once and accounting for no
more than about ten percent of activation variance; and they characterize it as a broadcast hub, based on
the unusually strong composition of J-lens directions with downstream component inputs - across model
depth and, via a subset of attention heads, across token positions - relative to ordinary directions.
Two scope qualifications are important. First, the authors explicitly do not claim to have found the full
architecture that global workspace theory ascribes to the brain: “there are no obviously separable input
processors, and the broadcast we document occurs within a single feedforward pass rather than through
recurrent loops.” Second, the experiments remain episode-bound at the model-internal level relevant here.
Some interventions affect later generated tokens, but the experiments do not demonstrate a model-internal
state that survives a separately initiated inference episode without being reconstructed from supplied
context. Both qualifications bear directly on the three conditions.

4. Reading the J-Space Against the Three Conditions
4.1 Persistent Internal Context: The Architectural Limitation and Its Scope
The threshold’s discussion of standard transformer inference already anticipates the relevant limitation.
Within a single inference pass, a transformer “carries forward an internally updated residue of prior
processing” and thus “plausibly satisfies a weak endogeneity condition.” Across separate episodes,
however, standard inference lacks a standing internal condition that varies with its prior inference-time
trajectory: once the token sequence is fixed, “the same current input yields the same internal state
regardless of prior trajectory.” Deployed continuity is often achieved by “offloading state into external
stores, tools, or human-maintained scaffolds,” but this does not show that the model-internal process
assessed in the threshold carries its own prior activity forward as a standing condition.
Gurnee et al. emphasize the corresponding architectural fact in motivating their comparison with
biological global-workspace models: a standard transformer lacks the recurrent loops usually invoked in
those theories, and extended deliberation often proceeds by writing intermediate results into token context
for later computation. This is relevant to Condition 1, but it should not be counted as independent
empirical confirmation of it. The point follows largely from the architecture; J-space’s distinctive
evidence lies in the causal interventions on representational content.
The diagnosis is also narrower than the claim that a transformer could never satisfy Condition 1. If a
deployed transformer were configured so that causally active model state genuinely persisted across the
temporal boundary chosen for assessment, rather than being reset and reconstructed from a supplied
record, Condition 1 would have to be reassessed for that system. The present verdict concerns the
standard episode-bounded model process used in the J-space experiments. This is why the distinction
cannot turn merely on whether an experimenter could reconstruct a state: deterministic recurrent systems
may also be reproducible in principle, yet their ordinary transition can still inherit a prior active state
directly.
Gurnee et al. also raise a possibility that bears on this diagnosis. In discussing recurrent processing theory,
they suggest that the computational property motivating a demand for recurrence may be serial processing
depth rather than recurrence as such, and that the early layers preceding workspace onset “may be a
functional analog for the role that sensory recurrence plays in the brain.” Condition 1 does not require
recurrence as such; it asks whether causally active organization is carried through the relevant transitions
of the assessed process. Serial depth may contribute weak intra-pass carryover, but it does not by itself
establish persistence across the episode boundary relevant to the present classification.
The strongest apparent counterexample comes from post-training. Gurnee et al. report that post-training
installs what they call the Assistant’s point of view in J-space. A post-trained model, unlike its pretrained
base, registers evaluative reactions to user situations - for example, unsafe, dangerous, and WARNING
while still parsing an overdose report - and shows signs of monitoring its own behavior, including a
distinctive BUT signal when a prefilled response commits it to an option it would not itself have chosen,
as well as markers that thought suppression has failed.
Taken at face value, this resembles the standing, system-relative orientation Condition 1 asks for. But
Condition 1 asks not merely whether the parameters encode a stable disposition; it asks whether the
current internal condition varies with the system’s own ongoing activity. Post-training can therefore
install a highly reliable disposition to reconstruct an Assistant-relative evaluation from supplied context
without showing that an evaluation formed in one episode is carried as the active causal starting point of a
separately initiated later episode. The Assistant-perspective result enriches the content of the bounded
near-case without dislodging Condition 1 for the model process assessed here.

4.2 Differentiated Internal Possibility: Causal Differentiation Without Demonstrated
Maintenance
Before the J-space report appeared, the threshold paper proposed a specific empirical handle on Condition
2: “hold the input fixed, perturb an internal direction, and test whether the downstream continuation space
is durably reorganized across multiple steps rather than merely shifted for the next token.” It also
cautioned that the missing evidence was whether such perturbations reveal internally maintainable
positions that reorganize continuation space in the required sense.
The J-space paper’s causal-intervention experiments closely match the causal-differentiation part of that
test. In one example, swapping the lens vector for a planned rhyme word (fight) with an unrelated one
(light) mid-couplet changes not only the next token but the remainder of the line, from “coming fight” to
“morning light.” In a systematic multi-hop reasoning evaluation, swapping an inferred intermediate
concept (for example, spider for ant) redirects the eventual answer in 54-70% of cases, depending on
model size. The intervention also takes effect at a processing depth a median of roughly seventeen percent
earlier than an equivalent swap of the answer itself, supporting the interpretation that the intermediate is
used rather than merely echoed.
These results establish structured causal differentiation, but the persistence component requires more care.
In the rhyme example, once altered words are emitted, later tokens can condition on the changed token
record; the remainder of the line therefore does not independently show that the perturbed internal
position was autonomously maintained. The result is strong evidence that J-space content is causally
upstream of later generation, not by itself evidence for maintenance under the system’s own dynamics.
The flexible-generalization test shows a different form of causal flexibility. Swapping a country’s
workspace vector (France for China) and holding the swap fixed across four downstream question
templates - capital, language, continent, and currency - causes the model to return the swapped-in
country’s corresponding answers across all four. But here the counterfactual vector is actually maintained
by the intervention procedure. That matters. The fact that an experimenter could reproduce a deterministic
recurrent state does not alter the causal route by which the system actually carries it; by contrast, when the
experimenter does hold a candidate position in place, the experiment does not establish autonomous
maintenance of that position. The France/China result therefore supports flexible causal use across
heterogeneous computations rather than the maintenance requirement of Condition 2.
Ablation experiments likewise show graded, task-differentiated effects: suppressing J-space content
leaves classification and extractive tasks largely intact while selectively degrading tasks that require recall
or free-form generation grounded in inferred content, including multi-hop reasoning, translation, and
sonnet writing. These findings strengthen the case that J-space differences have structured causal
consequences.
The results should nevertheless remain within the published rating. They occur within episode-bounded,
token-context-mediated processing, and the interventions do not show that an ordinarily reachable J-space
position can be maintained for multiple steps without being carried by the emitted record or externally
reimposed. The J-space paper’s limitations section adds a further qualification: as constructed, the J-lens
produces one vector per vocabulary token and can directly expose only concepts with single-token names;
the authors introduce template- and oracle-lens extensions for multi-token concepts in an appendix.
Several documented swap failures also occur when a concept’s lens vector is not strongly represented. J-

4.3 Closed-Loop Operativity: Role Differentiation Under Bounded Persistence
Condition 3 asks for more than wide causal influence; it requires differentiated influence across
distinguishable processing roles. The threshold individuates those roles interventionistically: two roles are
genuinely distinct when “admissible interventions can selectively alter the downstream consequences of
the current stance-position for one without correspondingly altering them for the other.” It therefore
distinguishes broad influence from cross-role operativity and calls for selective intervention tests.
The J-space paper’s selectivity experiments closely instantiate this test. In one series, the same latent
information - the language in which a passage is written - has dissociable causal roles across tasks.
Swapping the language’s J-lens representation reliably flips the model’s answer when it must explicitly
report the language or perform a flexible computation that depends on it, such as naming an author or
supplying a word for “hello.” The same intervention leaves continuation and anomaly-detection behavior
unaffected, even though the information appears in the J-space readout at comparable rates across all four
conditions. This is a clean case of selective intervention dissociating one position’s downstream
consequences across roles rather than producing a uniform shift in generation.
The alignment-auditing case studies are consistent with the same picture, but I do not rely on them here
because their decomposition into distinct roles is less controlled than in the selectivity experiments.
Of the three conditions, Condition 3’s role-differentiation component is the closest match to the J-space
experimental design. Distinguishing which computations do and do not “route through” a candidate
workspace is, in effect, a search for role-differentiated causal consequence. The selectivity experiments
therefore provide unusually direct evidence for that component of Condition 3. Because the conditions are
jointly necessary but diagnostically separable, this evidence remains meaningful even though Condition 2
is unresolved and Condition 1 is weak for the episode-bounded model process. It does not establish the
threshold as a whole or any stronger diachronic closure.

5. What This Changes, and What It Doesn't
The preceding analysis yields a specific claim: J-space changes the evidential status of the bounded nearcase, not its classification. For the episode-bounded model process assessed in the experiments, Condition
1 remains sufficient to block a positive threshold classification. That conclusion is conditional on the unit
of assessment: a transformer deployment that genuinely carries active model state across the relevant
temporal boundary would require a new Condition 1 analysis. Nothing in the J-space experiments
establishes such a change.
What changes is the evidential basis for Conditions 2 and 3. The threshold’s table records Condition 2 for
transformer inference as “unclear; at most weak” and Condition 3 as “partial/unclear,” for want of the
relevant causal-intervention evidence. J-space supplies unusually direct evidence that changing internal
representational position can reorganize later computation and that the causal use of a represented feature
can dissociate across roles. The latter strongly supports Condition 3’s role-differentiation component. The
former strengthens the causal-differentiation sub-diagnostic of Condition 2 but does not establish
autonomous maintenance, so the published overall rating for Condition 2 should remain unchanged.

6. What Would Move the Case
The threshold’s diagnostic table already identifies architecture classes in which Condition 1 is “plausibly
satisfied” and Condition 2 becomes the open question: long-term memory agents whose updates are
6

incorporated into an internally maintained condition rather than reintroduced as input, active-inference
agents with persistent generative models, and Dreamer-style world-model systems. A J-lens-style method
- computationally inexpensive, causally grounded, and well suited to swap-and-observe experiments would be natural to apply to such architectures. The relevant question would no longer be simply whether
a workspace exists at a given moment, but whether a candidate internal position is carried by the system’s
own ordinary dynamics and then organizes later processing.
Three comparisons would bear directly on that question. They address different evidential tasks and
should not be treated as interchangeable.
Input Equalization Test. Hold the current exogenous input fixed while placing the system in different
legitimately carried prior states or trajectories, then allow ordinary processing to continue without
replaying the divergent history through the input interface. If later processing differs, that is evidence that
some history-dependent causal state has been carried into the present. It is an existence test, not yet an
attribution test: another persistent quantity may covary with the manipulated history.
Continuation-Reconstruction Test. Compare native continuation with a fresh instance initialized only
from the record that the architecture ordinarily retains or re-supplies at the declared boundary. If the fresh
instance reproduces the same causally relevant state and subsequent trajectory, that supports a
reconstruction diagnosis across that boundary. If native continuation retains causally effective differences
that the ordinarily available record cannot reproduce, that supports system-carried orientation. Supplying
a complete artificial serialization of the hidden state or total causal history would change the question
rather than test it.
Active-Carryover Test. Perturb a nominated candidate persistent state when the perturbation is not
serialized into an output or other record that will later be supplied as input, then ask whether subsequent
processing preserves, revises, compensates for, or otherwise incorporates the perturbation under the
system’s ordinary dynamics. A positive result attributes continuing causal influence to the nominated
state itself. The current J-space interventions establish downstream effects within an episode, but they do
not show that a candidate position is maintained independently of the token record or external
intervention apparatus.

7. Concluding Remarks
The Standpoint Threshold and Gurnee et al.’s J-space paper are both deliberately modest about what their
results establish. The threshold is architectural rather than phenomenological, and satisfying all three
conditions would not by itself show that a system has subjective experience, semantic reference, or
normative standing. Gurnee et al. are equally explicit that access consciousness - the functional,
reportable, flexibly used notion their experiments target - is “a purely functional notion” whose relation to
phenomenal consciousness “is widely debated,” and they “take no position on this issue.” Neither paper
claims that any current system is conscious.
The point of applying the threshold’s diagnostic schema to J-space is not to produce a verdict on
consciousness. It is to test how a previously specified architectural framework handles a later mechanistic
result. The comparison sharpens three points that matter beyond this case. First, Condition 1 should be
assessed by the ordinary causal route of state carryover, not by whether an experimenter could reproduce
a deterministic state in principle; the present negative diagnosis is conditional on the episode-bounded
model process actually studied, not on every possible transformer serving regime. Second, J-space
provides strong evidence that internal representational differences can have structured downstream
consequences, but token-mediated or experimenter-maintained persistence should not be counted as

autonomous maintenance for Condition 2. Third, the selectivity experiments provide unusually direct
evidence for Condition 3’s cross-role differentiation diagnostic.
The resulting classification remains the original one for the J-space setting: a bounded near-case. J-space
strengthens the evidence relevant to Conditions 2 and 3 while Condition 1 remains sufficient to block a
positive threshold classification for the assessed process and Condition 2 overall remains “unclear; at
most weak.” The diagnosis is independent of whether J-space is best described as a substantial
workspace, a minimal workspace, or simply a structure supporting access-like functions (Chalmers,
2026). On this view, identifying a standpoint remains prior to asking which further consciousnessrelevant properties, if any, the system possesses.
